What a Real Janitorial Quality Inspection Looks Like

How professional cleaning contractors verify their own work — and how to tell if yours does.

Most Cleaning Contractors Do Not Inspect Their Own Work

This is not an accusation — it is a structural reality. A solo operator or a two-person crew working a tight route cannot simultaneously clean facilities and supervise the quality of that cleaning. Without a supervisory layer, quality control defaults to client complaints. The facility manager becomes the inspector, which means problems are caught only after they are visible and annoying enough to prompt a call.

A professional cleaning contractor has a documented quality inspection process. Here is what that process should look like.

The Written Scope as the Inspection Standard

A quality inspection without a written scope of work is subjective — the inspector is checking against their own opinion of what "clean" means. A quality inspection against a written scope is objective — each task either was or was not completed, and each area either meets the defined standard or it does not.

This is why the written scope matters beyond just defining the service. It is what makes quality checks meaningful and disputes resolvable. If the scope says "restrooms cleaned nightly, including fixture cleaning, floor mopping, mirror cleaning and restock," the inspection checklist writes itself.

Who Does the Inspection?

The inspection should be done by someone other than the crew who performed the cleaning. A crew member checking their own work is not an inspection — it is a confirmation that they remember doing the tasks. Effective quality control requires a separate supervisor or manager who walks the facility against the scope with fresh eyes.

Inspection frequency varies. For active contracts, unannounced supervisor visits once or twice per month are a reasonable standard. New contracts benefit from more frequent checks in the first 60 days while the crew is learning the facility.

What a Supervisor Checks

A walk-through quality inspection against a written scope typically covers:

  • Trash: All bins emptied, liners replaced, no overflow in lobbies or break rooms
  • Floors: Swept or vacuumed (no debris visible), mopped (no dried spill marks or streaks), no missed corners
  • Restrooms: Fixtures cleaned (toilet bowls, sinks, faucets, mirrors), floors mopped, dispensers stocked, odor absent
  • Surfaces: Desk surfaces wiped (where cleared), countertops, appliance exteriors in break room
  • Glass: Entry glass and interior partitions free of fingerprints and smears
  • Supply closet: Products organized, nothing running critically low

Inspection results are logged. Not in a way that becomes administrative overhead — a simple checklist with a date and any notes on items that needed follow-up is sufficient. The log provides a record if a pattern develops.

What Happens When Something Fails Inspection

A missed task at a quality check has two appropriate responses: correction at the next scheduled visit, or same-night correction if the issue is significant enough to warrant it. The facility manager is notified of the miss and of the plan to correct it.

A pattern of misses — the same task failing inspection repeatedly — is a training issue, a staffing issue or a scope issue. It requires a conversation, not just a correction. We treat patterns as process failures rather than crew failures until proven otherwise.

How to Ask a Contractor About Their Inspection Process

Ask these two questions directly: "Who performs your quality inspections, and how often?" and "What do you do when an inspection identifies a missed task?" The answers tell you whether the contractor has a real process or a verbal assurance.
