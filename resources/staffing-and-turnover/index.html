Crew Turnover and Why Your Building Keeps Getting New Cleaners

The cleaning industry has a real staffing problem. Here is what it means for your facility and what contractors do about it.

The Turnover Reality in Commercial Cleaning

Commercial cleaning has among the highest labor turnover rates of any service industry. Annual turnover rates above 100% are not unusual for cleaning contractors in the upper midwest — meaning the average crew member is replaced more than once per year. This is a structural feature of the industry, driven by entry-level wages, irregular hours, and the physical demands of the work.

For facility managers, turnover creates a predictable problem: the crew who learned your building, knew where things were, and understood your specific requirements is replaced by someone who does not. Quality dips, security protocols get re-explained, and the relationship resets. If this happens frequently enough, you are effectively training new cleaners at your own expense on a recurring basis.

Why Some Contractors Handle Turnover Better Than Others

Turnover is not equally distributed across contractors. Operations that pay above market, schedule consistently, provide reliable equipment and treat crew members professionally retain staff longer. Operations that cut corners on wages, equipment and crew management see constant churn.

The secondary factor is staffing depth. A contractor with 10 employees can absorb a crew member's absence with their own roster. A contractor with 3 employees cannot — they either skip the cleaning or call a day laborer who has never been in your building. The client absorbs the quality risk in the second scenario.

What Good Turnover Management Looks Like

A professional cleaning contractor handles turnover with a supervised transition rather than a surprise substitution. When a crew member who has been servicing your account leaves or is reassigned, the replacement is introduced and trained on your specific facility — not dropped in solo on the first night.

The written scope of work becomes critical here. A detailed scope means the new crew member has a documented standard to work from, not just word-of-mouth from the departing employee. The facility does not have to be re-explained from scratch.

What You Should Ask Your Current or Prospective Contractor

Three direct questions worth asking:

What is your crew turnover rate? A contractor with a real answer — even a high one — is more trustworthy than a contractor who claims turnover is not an issue. It is always an issue in this industry.

How do you handle a crew change on an active account? "Supervised transition" with notification is the correct answer. "We just send whoever is available" is not.

Do you use your own employees or subcontractors? Subcontractors are not inherently a problem, but if a contractor uses day labor or casual subcontractors to fill gaps, the turnover problem is amplified — those individuals have no relationship with the contractor's quality standards, training or client protocols.

Consistency and Why It Matters in Southern Dakota County

Facilities in a rural or semi-rural corridor like southern Dakota County have specific access requirements that matter more than they do in a dense urban market. Alarm codes, key box procedures, gate codes for facilities with secured lots, specific entry sequences for buildings with complex access systems — these details are learned by a consistent crew and lost with every substitution.

A crew that has serviced a facility for six months is faster, more thorough and produces fewer access incidents than one that arrived last week. Consistency is not a luxury; it is a direct cost factor.
